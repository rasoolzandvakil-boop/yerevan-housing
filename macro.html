Armenia · Macroeconomic backdrop

Strong Growth, One Concentrated Risk

Armenia enters 2026 with momentum: 7.2 percent growth in 2025, projections tightly clustered at 5.3–5.7 percent for 2026–27, record reserves, and positive outlooks from all three rating agencies. The soft spot is external — and the macro risk map is the housing risk map.

Growth and projections

Real GDP grew 12.6 percent in 2022, 8.3 in 2023, 5.9 in 2024, and 7.2 percent in 2025 (accelerating to 9.8 percent in Q4, with construction up 21 percent). Activity ran +7.7 percent through July 2026. The 2026/27 forecast range is tight: IMF 5.3/5.5 (its June 2026 program review says "around 5¼"), World Bank 5.3/5.1, EBRD 5.5/5.5, ADB 5.5/5.7, Fitch 5.2, Moody's 5.0–5.5, the government's budget 5.4 — all converging toward roughly 5 percent over the medium term. The IMF raised its estimate of potential growth from 4.5 to about 5 percent in the December 2025 Article IV, with 2026–27 above potential partly on the opening of the Amulsar gold mine. Nominal wages rose 5.6 percent in 2025 and registered employment 4.6 percent — a supportive but no longer extraordinary demand backdrop for housing.

Inflation and money

After averaging 0.3 percent in 2024 and 3.3 percent in 2025, inflation printed 4.2–5.3 percent year on year through mid-2026 against a 3 percent target, with food up 8.6 percent. Forecasts put 2026 at 3.6 percent (IMF) to 4.6 percent (World Bank), converging to target by 2027–28. The CBA cut its refinancing rate from a 10.75 percent peak (December 2022) to 6.50 percent (December 2025) and has held since; the IMF advises being "prepared to raise." Practical implication for property: dram mortgage rates of ~12.5 percent are unlikely to fall soon — local leverage stays expensive, cash buyers stay advantaged.

The external accounts — the soft spot

The current account swung from a 0.7 percent-of-GDP surplus in 2022 to deficits of 4.6 percent in 2024 and 5.4–6.7 percent in 2025 (the IMF's latest figure is 6.7). Headline exports fell 36 percent in 2025 — entirely the unwind of the extraordinary gold-and-diamond re-export arbitrage with Russia, which peaked at US$15.4 billion of precious-metals trade in 2024 and collapsed when Russia scrapped its gold export duty; excluding precious metals, exports grew 8.9 percent. Remittance-type inflows remain large (US$6.0 billion gross, US$1.6 billion net in 2025) and Russia-heavy (~65 percent). Net FDI fell to 0.3 percent of GDP in 2024, a historic low, on Russian divestment — a US$3.5 billion U.S.-linked AI data-center project is a prospective offset. The cushion has never been thicker: record reserves of US$5.2 billion at end-2025, US$5.7–6.9 billion by mid-2026, and a dram up roughly 28 percent against the dollar since 2021 (365 in August 2026).

Fiscal and ratings

Deficits of 3.7 percent of GDP in 2024–25 came in well under budget; the 2026 budget targets 4.5 percent while cutting defense spending 15 percent — the first cut since the 2020 war, a dividend of the August 2025 Washington peace declaration. Debt sits at 49–51 percent of GDP, peaking near 52 percent in 2028 on IMF projections, comfortably under the 60 percent fiscal rule. A precautionary IMF Stand-By Arrangement (SDR 128.8 million, December 2025, undrawn) provides insurance. Fitch (BB−), Moody's (Ba3), and S&P (BB−) all moved to positive outlooks in July–August 2026.

Risks — one map, not two

The IMF's adverse scenario — a geopolitical shock cutting remittances 45 percent — takes 2026 growth to 3.0 percent, pushes debt above 60 percent of GDP, and drains reserves toward US$2.6 billion. Russia accounts for roughly two-fifths of trade and tourists and two-thirds of transfers, and the marginal buyer and renter of central Yerevan property are disproportionately non-resident. Armenian macro risk and Yerevan property risk do not diversify each other — they are one bet, taken once. Upside is equally identifiable: implementation of the peace treaty and TRIPP corridor, a Türkiye border opening, and the EU-accession process launched in March 2025.
