Yerevan · Prices, transactions, districts

Boom, Pause, Re-acceleration

A decade of quiet, a migration- and subsidy-fueled boom in 2022–24, a flat 2025 once the subsidy expired in Yerevan, and renewed growth from Q2 2026 — with the momentum now in the mid-ring rather than the prestige center.

The price arc

The cadastre's Yerevan average stood at AMD 268,000 per sq m in early 2013 and only AMD 330,000–350,000 in 2020–21 — essentially a decade of stagnation in dram terms. The 2022 Russian influx lifted prices about 14 percent citywide; 2023 brought a pause; and 2024 delivered +8.8 percent to AMD 426,800 as buyers rushed to beat the mortgage tax-refund cutoff (Yerevan transactions +32 percent, December 2024 the strongest month in a decade, mortgage growth peaking at 37 percent). In 2025 the market went flat: +0.3 percent for the year, Kentron down 1.4 percent, Arabkir down 1.75 percent, pockets of the center down 4–7 percent — the IMF noted July 2025 as the first year-on-year decline in 50 months. From Q2 2026 the central bank reports renewed increases of 1–1.5 percent a month.

Sources: State Real Estate Cadastre Committee via press republications; Central Bank of Armenia.

The 2025 stagnation was specific to Yerevan apartments: apartments outside Yerevan rose 9.4 percent (regional cities +16.4 percent), and individual houses rose 17.4 percent on the year and 27.2 percent in Q4 (CBA RPPI) — the still-live regional tax refunds at work. In dollar terms the run-up is larger than the dram figures suggest: a dollar-based buyer faced both the price rise and ~25 percent currency appreciation since 2021.

The policy engine: the mortgage tax-refund program

Since 2015, salary income tax has been refundable against mortgage interest on new-build purchases from developers — cumulative refunds of AMD 299 billion to over 70,000 beneficiaries, costing 0.7 percent of GDP in 2024. The ADB judged it "had the unintended consequence of further inflating housing prices." The phase-out is geographic: central Yerevan lost eligibility in 2022–23, all of Yerevan on January 1, 2025; the four regions ringing the capital lose it January 1, 2027, and the rest in 2029.

Its footprints are in every series: the 2024 rush, the 2025 Yerevan stall, mortgage growth falling from 37 to 13 percent, the 16 percent regional surge — and the collapse of the new-construction pipeline in Kentron and Arabkir, which is the medium-term scarcity argument for quality central stock. Watch late 2026 for anticipatory buying in the four regions before the 2027 cutoff.

What the specialists say

  • IMF: overvaluation of 8–25 percent (2023, borrowing-capacity approach); market "now cooling" (Dec 2025); subsidy phase-out credited with the cool-down.
  • CBA: 16–25 percent above fundamentals (2022–23); 2024 completions doubled — excess-supply and credit-loss warnings; price-to-rent deterioration flagged as declining investment attractiveness.
  • ADB (2025): prices +100 percent since 2014; the bottom three income deciles effectively excluded; market "may be approaching the peak of its cycle."
  • Local consensus: soft landing — 3–7 percent annual growth in prime areas for 2026–27 (SIEVN), demand sustained through 2026 (Grant Thornton), flat real prices (realtors' association). The skeptics worry about the credit boom, not a crash.
  • Seismic screening: 45 percent of Yerevan's buildings are rated highly earthquake-susceptible; only ~5 percent retrofitted. The premium on post-2010s construction by reputable developers is partly a safety premium — and rational.
